# Honors Discrete Mathematics - course reference

**EN.553.172 - Fall 2026**

## Course scope

Discrete objects (sets, relations, permutations, graphs) and their connections
with abstract algebra. Goals: develop problem-solving and proof techniques.

- Dr. Su Ji is identified in the previously imported tentative schedule;
  this syllabus does not give the instructor's name or email.
- Instructor and TA office hours will be posted on Canvas.
- If scheduled instructor office hours do not work, email the instructor to
  arrange another time.
- Meeting times, classroom, TA names, and contact emails are not specified
  in this syllabus; check Canvas/SIS.

## Textbooks and proof-writing resources

- **Required:** Edward Scheinerman, *Mathematics: A Discrete Introduction*,
  **3rd edition**, ISBN **9780840049421**.
- **Optional:** Stephen Lovett, *Abstract Algebra, a first course*.
  Available online through the [JHU library](https://catalyst.library.jhu.edu/permalink/01JHU_INST/t3c16/alma991060496556107861).
- **Supplemental:** Ted Sundstrom, *Mathematical Reasoning: Writing and Proof*.

## Homework policy

- Typically one problem set each week.
- **Actual due dates are posted on Canvas and Gradescope.** Dates vary on
  exam weeks. The syllabus supplies no fixed weekday or time of day.
- Submit on **Gradescope**. **Late homework is not accepted.** No grace
  period or extension procedure is specified.
- Homework collectively contributes **15%** of the course grade.
- The existing 13 pset files come from the separate tentative schedule;
  this PDF does not confirm their individual dates or assigned problems.

### Submission format and presentation

- Scan homework and convert it to **PDF**.
- Scans must be **easily readable**.
- Select the page(s) containing **each problem** when uploading to Gradescope.
  The syllabus says graders cannot grade work without this information.
- Communicate logical steps clearly and justify every step, especially in proofs.
- **No numerical file-size limit is stated.**
- No specified page dimensions, page-count limit, DPI, color/grayscale rule,
  margins, font size, or filename convention.
- Regular homework has no stated LaTeX requirement; perfect proofs do.
  The syllabus does not expressly address tablet exports or typed regular homework.
- These are this course's requirements; LADS has its own scanning policy.

### Homework curve

The syllabus removes **100 points from the total possible homework points**
when computing the homework percentage, capped at 100%.

If S is the earned point total and T is the possible point total, the stated
examples correspond to H = min(100, 100*S/(T-100)), for T > 100.
This is a derived formula expressing the examples, not a dropped-assignment rule.

- 750 earned out of 900 possible becomes 750/800 = **93.75%**.
- 850 earned out of 900 possible is capped at 800/800 = **100%**.

### Collaboration

Discussing problems with friends and classmates is welcome. Write and submit
your own proofs and solutions, show your own work, and do not copy answers.

## Grading - two nested higher-of calculations

First compute the **in-class exam score E** as the higher of:

- 20% Exam 1 + 35% Exam 2 + 45% Exam 3.
- 30% Exam 1 + 35% Exam 2 + 35% Exam 3.

Then compute the **course score** as the higher of the two columns below:

| Component | Scheme A | Scheme B |
|---|---|---|
| In-class exam score E | 55% | 45% |
| Comprehensive final | 20% | 30% |
| Curved homework score | 15% | 15% |
| Participation and discussion | 5% | 5% |
| Perfect proofs | 5% | 5% |
| Total | 100% | 100% |

Use percentage scores consistently. The selected in-class formula and
selected course formula both depend on the student's scores; individual
in-class exams do not have one fixed course-grade weight.
Exams plus the final collectively account for **75%** under either scheme.

No letter-grade thresholds, overall course curve, or detailed
participation/attendance scoring rules are supplied.

## Academic honesty and AI policy

- Submitted writing and calculations must be your own.
- AI is encouraged for brainstorming, refining ideas, finding reliable
  sources, and checking grammar; outputs may be inaccurate, incomplete,
  biased, or otherwise problematic. You are responsible for submissions.
